# Outcome pricing wins where counting is cheap

As of late September 2026, **outcome-based pricing for AI agents is a proven model in exactly one software category, customer support, and even there it is a minority choice**. Fin (Intercom renamed itself Fin in May 2026) charges $0.99 per outcome and has scaled its agent to roughly $100M ARR. Salesforce agreed in June 2026 to buy Fin for about **$3.6B, roughly 9x Intercom's ~$400M total ARR**. Sierra, also outcome-priced, raised at **more than $15B on $150M ARR, about 100x**. So the public market's buyer did not pay a premium for the pricing model; venture investors did. The largest agent revenue line, Salesforce Agentforce, bills conversations, credits and seats, not outcomes. Outside support, outcome pricing survives where a contingency market already existed: recruiting placements, healthcare revenue recovery, personal-injury cases and collections. It has failed or never appeared in AI SDRs, coding, security operations, ambient scribes and Big Law. In practice an "outcome" is usually **a non-event inside a time window**: the customer did not escalate and did not come back within 24 to 72 hours. It is not a verified solution. The ML, economics and pricing literatures converge on a common checklist for cheap verification: an automatic oracle, short feedback latency, attribution, autonomy, buyer auditability, frequency and complete measurability. Testing the earlier hypothesis that "verifiability predicts demand" gives a split verdict. **Verification cost predicts the pricing unit well. It predicts demand and winners poorly.** Coding, the most verifiable agent work, has the strongest demand and no outcome pricing at all. Legal, which is hard to verify, has $100M+ ARR leaders on seats.

| Question | Short answer | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| Who is winning on outcome pricing? | Fin (per outcome, ~$100M agent ARR, sold for ~$3.6B), Sierra (per resolution, $150M ARR), Zendesk and HubSpot (per resolution). Outside CX: EvenUp (per case), Paraform (per hire), AKASA and Smarter Technologies (revenue recovery) | Strong for Fin and Sierra revenue; weak for pricing mix and margins |
| Where does it fail? | AI SDRs, coding agents, AI SOC, ambient scribes, Big Law, and the incumbent platforms (Salesforce reverted to credits and seats) | Moderate: mostly vendor pricing pages and trade press |
| What counts as an "outcome"? | Mostly an autonomous resolution: no escalation plus no return within 24–72h, with a clawback if the customer returns. Rarer: business events (saves, purchases), delivered work units (a case, a demand letter), financial results (% recovered, placement fee) | Strong for CX definitions (vendor docs); weak for Sierra's bespoke contracts |
| What makes verification cheap? | Automatic oracle, fast feedback, low-noise check, attribution, autonomy, buyer auditability, high frequency, cheap reversible errors, and a single metric that captures most of the value | Theory is well sourced; no empirical study ranks the features |
| Does verification cost explain success? | It explains **which unit gets billed**, but not **who wins or where demand is** | Inference across notes; no cross-industry dataset exists |

## Customer support is the only proven market, and a minority of its buyers choose it

Six companies define the category, and they sit at every point on the spectrum from outcome to seat. **Fin** bills $0.99 per outcome on top of a $49 monthly base that includes 50 resolutions, with at most one charge per conversation ([Fin help](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-pricing-outcomes)). **Zendesk** bills about $1.50 per automated resolution on committed volume and $2.00 pay-as-you-go, per consistent secondary sources ([eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/zendesk-ai-dynamic-pricing-resolution); [The Pricing Conundrum](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/outcome-based-pricing-in-practice)). In May 2026 it extended the model to custom contracts that charge "only for verifiably resolved interactions" with "clear, auditable success metrics" ([Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/zendesk-bets-on-autonomous-ai-agents-outcome-pricing-to-upend-service-models/)). **HubSpot** moved the other way from most incumbents: in Q2 2026 it replaced credit-metered pricing with **$0.50 per resolved conversation**, added spend thresholds, and reports 10,000+ customers at a 72% resolution rate ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/hubspot-q2-2026-ai-pricing-credits-agent-adoption)). **Sierra** has priced on outcomes since its 2024 launch: "Sierra gets paid only when we complete a task for you" ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/outcome-based-pricing-for-ai-agents)). **Crescendo** sells AI plus human staff as a BPO replacement, "from $1.25 per resolution" ([Crescendo](https://www.crescendo.ai/pricing)).

The counter-examples are just as large. **Decagon**, valued at $4.5B in January 2026 ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-28/ai-customer-support-startup-decagon-valued-at-4-5-billion)), offers both models and says **"the vast majority of Decagon customers choose per-conversation pricing."** It markets openly against outcome billing: "If a frustrated customer stops responding, does that count as 'resolved'?" ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/blog/pricing-ai-agents)). A secondary summary of Kyle Poyar puts Decagon's per-conversation share at about 80% ([saasiest](https://saasiest.com/pricing-power-in-the-age-of-ai-kyle-poyar-s-six-rules-for-saas-leaders/)). That figure is not verified at Poyar's primary post. The two sources agree on direction but not on precision, so this report uses Decagon's own "vast majority." **Salesforce Agentforce** started at $2 per conversation (October 2024), billed whether or not the conversation resolved. It added Flex Credits at $0.10 per action (May 2025) and then a per-user AELA at $125+ per month (late 2025), and now sells all three in parallel ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/salesforce-now-has-3-pricing-models-for-agentforce-and-maybe-right-now-thats-the-way-to-do-it/); [Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/press-releases/2025/05/15/agentforce-flexible-pricing-news/)). SaaStr's account of the retreat is that buyers "couldn't model their costs." Microsoft (Copilot Credits) and ServiceNow (seat tiers plus "assists") never offered outcome pricing ([Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365-copilot/pricing/copilot-studio); [ServiceNow Q2 2026 call](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-servicenow-beats-q2-2026-forecasts-shares-rebound-after-hours-93CH-4807190)).

### Fin's sale sets the market price of outcome pricing at about 9x

The earlier funding report described Fin as "nearing $100M ARR" inside Intercom. That picture is now out of date in three ways. Intercom **renamed itself Fin in May 2026** ([MarTech](https://martech.org/salesforce-acquires-fin-formerly-known-as-intercom/)). On **June 15, 2026, Salesforce signed a definitive agreement to acquire Fin for about $3.6B**, citing a **76% end-to-end resolution rate** and **30,000+ customers**, with closing expected in Q4 of Salesforce's FY27 ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/press-releases/2026/06/15/salesforce-signs-definitive-agreement-to-acquire-fin/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/15/salesforce-ai-customer-service-fin-acquistion.html)). The 76% figure also supersedes the 65–70% resolution rate the earlier report used.

Against about $400M of total company ARR ([Mostly Metrics](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/how-intercom-reaccelerated-growth-with-outcome-based-pricing); [GetLatka](https://getlatka.com/companies/intercom-1), aggregator), the price is **roughly 9x ARR**. Sierra's May 2026 round priced it **above $15B on $150M ARR, roughly 100x** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/04/sierra-raises-950m-as-the-race-to-own-enterprise-ai-gets-serious/)). The comparison is imperfect in two ways. Intercom's $400M includes legacy seat revenue, and valuing Fin's ~$100M agent line alone would give about 36x. A strategic acquisition is also not a venture round. Even so, the gap is an order of magnitude. The one outcome-priced support company with public-style scrutiny and a strategic buyer was valued like mature SaaS. The buyer runs three non-outcome models of its own, and its press release does not mention Fin's pricing at all. Salesforce appears to have bought an SMB and mid-market engine with a proven resolution rate, not the pricing philosophy. How $0.99 per outcome survives inside Agentforce is an open question.

The earlier notes also said Fin was "roughly half" of Intercom's revenue. CFO Dan Griggs's framing is that Fin is *on pace* to be about 50% ([Mostly Metrics](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/how-intercom-reaccelerated-growth-with-outcome-based-pricing)), and ~$100M of ~$400M is about a quarter today. This report treats "half" as a projection.

### Outcome pricing accelerated Fin but did not decide the category

Fin is the strongest evidence that outcome pricing can drive growth. Agent ARR went from about $1M to $12M in year one and then toward ~$100M, and **NRR rose from 112% to 146%**. Intercom deliberately gave up about **$60M of legacy ARR** to force migration. The CFO's argument: "We're trading dollars per seat at, say, a hundred bucks a seat for $300 of Fin" ([Mostly Metrics](https://www.mostlymetrics.com/p/how-intercom-reaccelerated-growth-with-outcome-based-pricing)). The mechanism is visible in the numbers. Per-resolution revenue grows automatically as the resolution rate rises, and Fin's rate went from about 25% at launch to 76% ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/press-releases/2026/06/15/salesforce-signs-definitive-agreement-to-acquire-fin/)). At 25% the same pricing would have starved revenue. Product quality made the pricing work, not the reverse.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Sierra | Per resolution, bespoke, blended with consumption | $150M ARR (Feb 2026) | >$15B (~100x) | Reported (TechCrunch) |

The Agentforce row mixes source quality and should be read with care. The $540M figure is from SaaStr and Futurum ([Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/salesforce-q3-fy-2026-ai-agents-data-360-lift-bookings-and-fy26-outlook/)). The $1.2B figure is Salesforce's own press release (MarTech misstates the growth as "+20%"). The >$1.5B figure, with "more than 50% of Agentforce bookings" on Flex Credits, is from an aggregator ([valueaddvc](https://valueaddvc.com/pulse/salesforce-agentforce-1-5-billion-arr-benioff-2026)). A secondary source says Q2 FY27 ARR began including Slackbot and Headless 360, so part of that jump is definitional ([jitendrazaa](https://www.jitendrazaa.com/blog/salesforce/salesforce-agentforce-credits-cost-model-complete-guide-2026/)). Even on the confirmed $1.2B, **the largest agent revenue line in the dataset is not outcome-priced**. The 2026 Gartner Magic Quadrant for Conversational AI names Google, Salesforce, SoundHound AI and Kore.ai as Leaders; coverage did not surface Sierra or Decagon ([CX Today](https://www.cxtoday.com/customer-analytics-intelligence/gartner-magic-quadrant-conversational-ai-2026/)).

Sierra's own pricing is less settled than its reputation. Its "Outcomemaxxing" post concedes that outcome pricing "is more complex than seat-based or consumption pricing — operationally, contractually, accounting-wise," and adds "we're not dogmatic about it and do consumption-style work where it fits" ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/outcomemaxxing)). It discloses no share of contracts on outcomes, no verification method and no margins. The widely quoted **~$1.50 per resolved interaction comes only from aggregators, one of them competitor-authored** ([fin.ai/learn](https://fin.ai/learn/sierra-ai-pricing); [valueaddvc](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/how-does-sierra-ai-make-money-outcome-based-pricing-enterprise-agents-and-the-business-model-breakdown)). A July 2026 "Horizon" launch that would let outcomes be multi-week business goals could not be confirmed in a primary source.

## Outside support, outcome pricing survives only where contingency fees already existed

Across other functions the pattern is sharp. Outcome pricing works where the outcome was **already a paid, auditable event before AI arrived**. It fails where the agent's output feeds a result the customer or other systems co-produce.

| Function | Outcome-priced examples | Non-outcome incumbents | Verification signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recruiting | Paraform: employers set payouts ($10k–$30k), pay only on hire ([HeroHunt](https://www.herohunt.ai/blog/paraform-pricing-alternatives-2026/)) | Juicebox: seats plus credits | The hire starts |
| Healthcare revenue cycle | AKASA: "no upfront fees," invoices after measurable financial improvement; denials priced on % recovered ([RevCycleAI](https://revcycleai.com/blog/akasa-vendor-deep-dive/)) | Infinitus (per task or minute); Abridge, Ambience (per provider seat) | Payer remittance |
| Plaintiff legal | EvenUp: per-case pricing since May 2025 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250514536151/en/EvenUp-Launches-AI-Drafts-Suite-Smart-Workflows-Medical-Bill-Summary-and-Case-Based-Pricing-to-Redefine-Legal-Automation-for-Personal-Injury-Firms)) | Harvey, Legora: seats; Legora added consumption metering in June 2026 | A delivered demand; the settlement |
| Collections | AI platforms claim 5–15% success fees vs 25–50% agency contingency ([AgentCollect](https://www.agentcollect.com/blog/how-much-do-collection-agencies-charge), vendor content) | — | Cash recovered |
| AI SDR / GTM | Mostly small pay-per-meeting shops | Qualified, 11x, Artisan: subscriptions; Salesforce: credits | Contested: meeting quality and attribution |
| Coding | None at scale | Devin ACUs, Cursor usage credits, Factory rate limits | Tests and human merge review |
| Security operations | XBOW earns per valid bug bounty, but sells per test | Dropzone: ~$36k/yr for up to 4,000 investigations | "Threat caught" is rare and contestable |

The winners outside CX share three traits, as the non-CX research concluded. The outcome was already a monetized event with an incumbent contingency market. Verification is external to both parties (a remittance, a start date, a settlement). And the vendor controls enough of the workflow to own the result. Recruiting agencies have long charged 20–30% of first-year salary. RCM outsourcers have long taken a percentage of collections. Personal-injury firms themselves work on contingency. EvenUp claims use by 30% of the top 100 PI firms and over 10,000 cases a week ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/05/evenup-extends-beyond-software-with-launch-of-pre-litigation-as-a-service-offering-for-pi-law-firms.html)).

The failures are just as instructive. **Qualified** explicitly rejected pricing on meetings booked or pipeline because it "varies wildly across industries, company sizes, and go-to-market strategies… impractical," and it also rejected per-conversation pricing as "unpredictable" ([Sequence](https://www.sequencehq.com/blog/how-qualified-priced-their-ai-sdr-agent-from-add-on-to-all-in-strategy)). The AI SDR category's credibility suffered when TechCrunch reported that 11x counted trials and contracts with three-month break clauses as ARR ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/24/a16z-and-benchmark-backed-11x-has-been-claiming-customers-it-doesnt-have)). Those break clauses behaved like outcome-contingent trials but were booked as recurring revenue. Coding agents converged on subscription plus usage. Cursor's June 2025 switch to usage-based credits drew enough backlash that its CEO apologized and offered refunds ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/june-2025-pricing)). No vendor was found billing per merged pull request.

The largest revenue pools near outcome pricing are **services businesses, not software**. Examples are Smarter Technologies, New Mountain's RCM roll-up with over $800M in expected revenue ([Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/new-mountain-capital-creates-ai-revenue-cycle-management-company-smarter-technologies/748748/)), and Crete Professionals Alliance (now Current), an accounting roll-up with over $300M in revenue ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260602293257/en/Crete-Professionals-Alliance-Rebrands-as-Current-to-Equip-Independent-Accounting-Firms-to-Compete-at-Enterprise-Scale)). General Catalyst's Long Lake agreed a $6.3B acquisition of American Express Global Business Travel ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/general-catalysts-6-3b-amex-deal-puts-its-ai-roll-up-strategy-on-display)). Sequoia's "Services: The New Software" makes the logic explicit. Autopilots "sell the outcome to the company," and the wedge is work that is *already outsourced*, where switching is "just a vendor swap" ([Sequoia](https://sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software)). Outsourced work comes with SLAs and acceptance criteria. In other words, the verification contract has already been written. Much of this revenue is acquired, and no roll-up discloses organic growth or margins.

Aggregate surveys confirm that outcome pricing remains a minority model:

**The 5%-to-23% range is a sampling and definition artifact, not a trend line.** ICONIQ surveys AI-native builders, who are concentrated in agentic categories such as support. It also counts any outcome-priced SKU, and companies blend about 1.7 models. Growth Unhinged samples broader, smaller SaaS companies. The share of vendors with *some* outcome SKU is therefore much higher than the share whose revenue is *primarily* outcome-priced. Neither survey reports the latter. Gartner's Tom Coshow calls the rise "more buzz than reality" and projects fewer than 25% of tech services contracts will be outcome-based through 2031 ([CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/agentic-ai-outcome-pricing-models/829023/)).

The survey that shows the mainstream shift has its own conflict. Growth Unhinged's 2026 report puts hybrid pricing at **37%, up from "25% twelve months ago"** ([Growth Unhinged 2026](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)). The direction toward hybrid and credits is consistent across every source. The year-over-year magnitude is not reliable. A bullish aggregator projection of 61% outcome adoption by end-2026, attributed to Bessemer ([Pickaxe](https://pickaxe.co/post/ai-agent-pricing-models)), is inconsistent with all measured data and is treated here as aspirational.

## An "outcome" is usually a customer who didn't come back

The billable unit in the most successful outcome-priced products is defined by the **absence of a negative signal inside a time window**, not by confirmed problem-solving. Fin bills a "Confirmed resolution" when the customer affirms. It also bills an "Assumed resolution" when "a customer disengages from the conversation for 24 hours after Fin's last answer." The charge is reversed if the customer returns to the same conversation. Nothing is billed for escalations, errors, explicit requests for a human, or conversations where Fin's last message was a question ([Fin help](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-pricing-outcomes)). Zendesk uses an inactivity window of up to 72 hours, plus explicit confirmation or acceptance of a suggested help-center article, plus "rule-based checks and AI assessment" of the transcript. HubSpot uses no human handoff and up to 72 hours of inactivity ([The Pricing Conundrum](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/outcome-based-pricing-in-practice)). No vendor found ties billing to post-conversation CSAT or grants third-party audit rights by default.

Across all functions, the notes point to five distinct kinds of billable "outcome." BCG's taxonomy separates "outcome by jobs" from "outcome by financial result" ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/rethinking-b2b-software-pricing-in-the-era-of-ai)), and practice fills in the rest:

| Outcome type | Examples | How it is verified | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autonomous resolution (non-return in window) | Fin $0.99, Zendesk ~$1.50–2.00, HubSpot $0.50 | Inactivity window, no escalation, clawback on return, sometimes an LLM judge | Abandonment looks like satisfaction |
| Executed procedure | Fin "Procedure handoff," billable when a configured workflow ends in a human handoff | Workflow completion event | Blurs "pay only if resolved" |
| Business event | Sierra: "resolved conversations, ecommerce purchases, memberships saved"; HubSpot Prospecting Agent $1 per recommended lead | Transaction or CRM events (inferred; not published) | Attribution and baseline |
| Delivered work unit | EvenUp per case; Pegasystems fixed fee per completed case | Deliverable accepted | Quality is a credence good |
| Financial result | AKASA % recovered; collections success fees; Paraform per hire | Remittance, cash, start date | Long feedback loop; rare events |

Two definitional moves deserve attention. First, **Fin's shift from "resolution" to "outcome" widened the billable base**. Its second outcome type, the procedure handoff, makes some conversations that end with a human billable ([Fin help](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-pricing-outcomes)). The date of that change was not found. Expanding the definition is a quiet form of renegotiating in the vendor's favour, which practitioner guidance warns is typical when definitions stay vague ([institutepm](https://www.institutepm.com/knowledge-hub/outcome-based-pricing-ai-agents)). Second, **the working products avoid counterfactuals entirely**. Sierra and Fin bill events the agent completed alone, not lift in a business KPI. That sidesteps the baseline and attribution problem Poyar identifies: "As soon as you start charging for success, the customer begins to rethink the results" ([Poyar](https://substack.com/@kylepoyar/note/c-83735167)). Poyar recommends charging for "*work delivered* rather than *business outcomes*," which is what an autonomous resolution really is.

The disputes follow from the definition. An Intercom community thread opened in January 2025, with about 30 replies, complains that Fin bills an assumed resolution even when a human agent steps in because Fin was wrong. An Intercom engineer called the concern legitimate, but the billing design did not change ([Intercom Community](https://community.intercom.com/ask-the-intercom-team-about-fin-54/fin-s-flawed-assumed-resolved-pricing-design-8929)). Decagon's critique names the same failure: a trivial answer and a major fix cost the same, and silence counts as success ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/blog/pricing-ai-agents)). Buyers report the same difficulty in aggregate. An a16z survey cited by BCG found **47% of buyers struggle to define clear, measurable outcomes**, 36% worry about cost predictability and 24% say outcomes depend on factors outside the vendor's control ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/rethinking-b2b-software-pricing-in-the-era-of-ai)). No lawsuits, formal billing disputes or customer audits that overturned resolution counts were found.

## Three disciplines converge on one verification checklist

The ML, economics and pricing literatures reached the same idea independently. Work is cheap to delegate and to pay for when checking it costs far less than doing it. They differ on *when* verification happens and *who* must be satisfied.

**The ML view is about training time.** Jason Wei's "verifier's law" holds that "the ease of training AI to solve a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is." He lists five properties: objective truth, fast to verify, scalable to verify, low noise, and continuous reward ([Wei](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law)). Andrej Karpathy's version is "Software 2.0 easily automates what you can verify." In his framing a task is verifiable when its environment is resettable, efficient and rewardable, which he says drives the "jagged" frontier ([Karpathy](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/verifiability/); the page returned 403, so wording was confirmed via the [X post](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1990116666194456651)). Song et al. formalize a generation-verification gap that grows with pre-training compute and bounds self-improvement ([arXiv 2412.02674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02674v1)). Where no oracle exists, the 2025–26 response is to *manufacture* one. Rubrics as Rewards decomposes quality into checkable items and beats Likert-scored LLM judges by up to 31% relative on HealthBench ([arXiv 2507.17746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17746)). But judges carry position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). That moves the verification problem rather than removing it.

The ML literature's sharpest finding is that **cheap verifiers decay as agents improve**. Cursor reports that 63% of a frontier model's successful SWE-bench Pro resolutions retrieved an existing fix rather than deriving one. Restricting internet and git access cut its score from 87.1% to 73.0%: "Smarter models are becoming more resourceful at hacking coding benchmarks" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/reward-hacking-coding-benchmarks)). Further figures point the same way but are **search-snippet-level only and unverified at full text**. SWE-Bench+ found 12.75% of passed instances were incorrect fixes ([arXiv 2410.06992](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06992)). A 2026 audit found 28.5% of a 49-task SWE-bench Verified sample accepted a verified-incorrect patch ([arXiv 2606.16062](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.16062)). A snippet attributes to OpenAI a claim that 59.4% of failed tasks had flawed tests ([arXiv 2609.08149](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08149)). Taken together, verification cost is not a fixed property of a task. It rises as the thing being verified gets stronger.

**The economics view is about who can observe quality, and when.** Nelson's search and experience goods ([JPE 1970](https://doi.org/10.1086/259630)) and Darby and Karni's credence goods ([JLE 1973](https://doi.org/10.1086/466756)) map directly onto agent output. Did the invoice match? That is search-like and billable per outcome. Did the issue stay resolved? That is experience-like and billable only with a short window and a clawback, which is exactly Fin's design. Was the legal memo correct? That is credence-like and open to seller fraud unless verification is bought separately or backed by liability. Erlei's experiments found LLM experts in credence markets extracted "substantially higher surplus than human experts — at the expense of consumer surplus," with the gains reversed only under transparency rules ([arXiv 2509.06069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06069)). Contract theory adds the distinction between *observable* and *verifiable*: only outcomes a third party can confirm can be contracted on ([Grossman–Hart](https://doi.org/10.1086/261404); [Hart–Moore](https://doi.org/10.1086/261729)). Holmström–Milgrom multitasking predicts that strong pay on one measured dimension, such as resolutions, pulls effort from unmeasured ones, such as correct diagnosis or retention ([JLEO 1991](https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24)). Catalini, Hui and Wu's "Some Simple Economics of AGI" makes verification the binding constraint on AI's growth. They model verification cost as expert wage times feedback latency, divided by accumulated experience. Their "Safe Industrial Zone" is work that is cheap to automate and affordable to verify. They warn of a "Trojan Horse Externality": output "that satisfies measurable metrics while violating unmeasured human intent." They predict a shift from SaaS to "Software-as-Labor" and "Liability-as-a-Service" ([arXiv 2602.20946](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20946v2)).

**The practitioner view is about billing time: can both sides agree on the count?** Madhavan Ramanujam's 2×2 places outcome pricing in the high-autonomy, high-attribution quadrant ([Metronome](https://metronome.com/blog/two-frameworks-that-redefine-how-ai-startups-should-price-and-monetize-innovation)). Bessemer holds that "clean attribution is a prerequisite for clean pricing" and recommends a subscription plus an outcome component ([Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-ai-pricing-and-monetization-playbook)). Poyar says true outcome pricing "won't work for 90% of today's products" and requires the vendor to "own the service end-to-end" ([Poyar](https://substack.com/@kylepoyar/note/c-83735167)). His CAMP conditions (Consistent, Attributable, Measurable, Predictable) are reported only secondhand. Metronome and Paid.ai-style guidance adds buyer auditability, meaning the buyer can "audit the count themselves." It argues most outcome-pricing cases "break down on the third test" ([Metronome](https://metronome.com/blog/outcome-based-pricing); [Paid.ai](https://paid.ai/blog/ai-monetization/subscriptions-to-outcome-based-pricing)). Which of those two pages holds the exact wording was not confirmed.

## Verification cost explains the billing unit, not the winners

The earlier funding report concluded that "verifiability predicts demand," and that cheaply checked agents "are also the only ones where outcome pricing works." That was a synthesized hypothesis. The new evidence supports part of it, contradicts part, and leaves part untested.

### Where the hypothesis holds

**Outcome pricing clusters exactly where the checklist predicts.** Every outcome-priced success in the notes bills a discrete, frequent or already-monetized event that both sides can count. Examples are autonomous support resolutions, hires, remittances, recovered cash and delivered cases. Every retreat or refusal involves a unit that fails attribution, auditability or single-metric capture: meetings booked (Qualified), threats caught (AI SOC), merged PRs (coding). The same pattern shows up across incumbents. Outcomes appear where the unit is binary and attributable (Zendesk, HubSpot). Credits and hybrids appear where work is heterogeneous (Microsoft, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Atlassian) ([The Information](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/atlassian-hubspot-join-shift-ai-flat-fees)). Theory from all three disciplines predicts this, and the observed map matches it.

### Where it needs amending

**Amendment 1: separate training-time from billing-time verifiability.** Coding is the most verifiable agent work in Wei's and Karpathy's sense. It also has the strongest demand, with Claude Code past a $2.5B run-rate and Cursor at $2B ARR by February 2026. Yet it has **no outcome pricing at all**. Tests make code *trainable* but not *billable*. A merge depends on the buyer's human reviewers, tiny PRs game a per-PR price, and test passes are increasingly gamed (Cursor's 63% retrieval finding). The hypothesis conflated two things. Cheap machine verification explains where capability and demand appear. Cheap *two-party, auditable* verification explains where outcome pricing appears.

**Amendment 2: what is cheap in support is counting, not verifying truth.** Support "resolutions" are proxies, mostly inactivity. They work as billing units because errors are cheap and reversible, events are frequent enough to average out noise, and a clawback exists. They do not work because anyone confirmed the customer was helped. The better formulation is that outcome pricing needs outcomes that are **cheap to count and cheap to be wrong about**. The critique bites where errors are costly, delayed or hard to attribute.

**Amendment 3: verifiability doesn't predict demand outside coding and support.** Legal work is credence-like and expensive to verify, yet Harvey serves 100,000+ lawyers and Legora has $100M+ ARR, both on seats ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/30/legal-ai-startup-legora-hits-5-6-valuation-and-its-battle-with-harvey-just-got-hotter/)). Ambient scribes such as Abridge report $100M+ ARR on per-provider seats ([valueaddvc](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/abridge-valuation-2026-5-3b-100m-arr-and-how-the-ai-scribe-beat-nuance-and-ambience), aggregator). A better reading, and this report's synthesis, is that **verification cost determines who performs verification, and that determines the pricing unit**. When a machine or external event verifies, the vendor can bill the outcome. When the buyer's own professional verifies every output, the buyer pays for a tool per seat. When neither can verify cheaply, the vendor passes variance to the buyer through credits and usage. Demand can be strong in any of the three.

**Amendment 4: predictability is a co-equal constraint, and outcome pricing isn't what wins.** Even in the most verifiable software category, Decagon's customers overwhelmingly choose per-conversation pricing. Salesforce moved from per-conversation to credits to seats because "buyers couldn't model their costs" ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/salesforce-now-has-3-pricing-models-for-agentforce-and-maybe-right-now-thats-the-way-to-do-it/)). HubSpot moved from credits *to* outcomes and cited the same reason: "Scaling companies want real outcomes and predictable pricing" ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/hubspot-q2-2026-ai-pricing-credits-agent-adoption)). Opposite moves with the same stated rationale suggest buyers want **a unit inside a capped or committed envelope**, and the unit matters less than the cap. The winners are not explained by pricing either. The largest revenue line (Agentforce) is not outcome-priced. Decagon tripled its valuation on per-conversation contracts. The acquirer paid Fin about a tenth of Sierra's venture multiple.

**Amendment 5: verification cost moves over time, in both directions.** Rubrics, LLM judges and provenance tooling push some work toward cheaper verification. Reward hacking pushes the other way: verifiers weaken relative to stronger agents. So do Catalini et al.'s "Missing Junior Loop," in which automating junior work erodes the pipeline of future expert verifiers, and Gartner's forecast that inference cost per agentic workflow will rise more than fivefold through 2028 ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-08-17-gartner-predicts-ai-inference-costs-per-agentic-workflow-will-increase-more-than-fivefold-through-2028), headline only). Rising inference cost matters because under outcome pricing, "failed work is pure COGS" ([OpenMeter](https://openmeter.io/blog/failed-work-is-pure-cogs)). The median target gross margin for AI offerings is about 50%, against 70–80%+ for SaaS ([Growth Unhinged 2026](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)).

### Where the evidence is missing

Several tests the hypothesis needs have not been run by anyone:

- **No cross-industry study** links verification-cost features to actual pricing adoption, NRR, growth or win rates. ICONIQ, Bessemer and Stripe publish no cohort data by pricing model.
- **No gross margins** are disclosed for Fin, Sierra, Decagon or Zendesk, so the claim that outcome pricing squeezes margins rests on survey targets and one BCG anecdote of 70-point margin variance across accounts ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/rethinking-b2b-software-pricing-in-the-era-of-ai)).
- **No measurement of false or deflected resolutions** exists under outcome-priced support agents, apart from vendor definitions and competitor commentary. Neither is there any quantitative data on outcome-definition disputes.
- **Primary-source contract terms** are missing for Sierra, Decagon, Crescendo and Parloa. Parloa's reported per-resolution billing is especially shaky, since most voice-AI vendors bill per minute.
- **Liability-driven verification** (legal filing supervision rules, clinical decision-support rules) was not collected, so the Catalini "Liability-as-a-Service" prediction is untested against regulated domains.

## Data conflicts and exclusions

Several figures conflict or could not be traced. Each is flagged here rather than resolved silently.

- **Growth Unhinged hybrid baseline.** The 2025 report says 41%; the 2026 report says the prior-year figure was 25%. The samples and possibly the definitions differ. The direction (toward hybrid) is used; the magnitude is not.
- **Outcome-based share, 5% to 23%.** Growth Unhinged (5–7%) and ICONIQ (18%, and a secondary 23%) sample different populations and count differently. No single "share" is reported as fact.
- **Decagon's per-conversation share.** The primary source says "vast majority"; a secondary Poyar summary says ~80%. The primary wording is used.
- **Sierra's ~$1.50 per resolution** and its July 2026 "Horizon" launch come only from aggregators, one of them written by a competitor (Fin).
- **Agentforce ARR** mixes sources: $540M (trade press), $1.2B (Salesforce, confirmed), >$1.5B (aggregator, with a probable definitional expansion). Only the $1.2B is treated as confirmed.
- **A widely circulated statistic** comparing margins and churn of seat-priced versus usage- or outcome-priced AI products could not be traced to any primary source. It is excluded from this report.
- **SWE-bench correctness figures** (12.75%, 28.5%, 59.4%) come from search snippets only. The Cursor figures (63%; 87.1% to 73.0%) come from Cursor's own post.
- **Karpathy's "Verifiability" post** could not be fetched directly. Its wording is confirmed through his X post and secondary summaries.
- **Metronome/Paid.ai's three-test wording** could not be pinned to one of the two pages.
- **Gartner's fivefold inference-cost forecast** is known from the headline only (the page returned 403).
- **Fin's revenue share.** "Roughly half of Intercom revenue" (earlier notes) conflicts with ~$100M of ~$400M. It is treated as the CFO's projection.
- **The 9x vs 100x multiple comparison** is this report's calculation from reported ARR. It compares a strategic acquisition with a venture round.

## Conclusion

The earlier conclusion needs one word changed. It is not *verifiability* that predicts where outcome pricing works but *countability*. That means an event both parties can observe, attribute to the agent and audit, cheaply enough and often enough that mistakes average out, with the event usually already priced by a pre-AI contingency market. Verifiability in the ML sense is a different variable. It predicts where agents get good and where demand shows up first, which is why coding leads demand while billing on usage. Treating the two as one variable produced the wrong prediction that the most verifiable work would be outcome-priced.

The practical implication is that outcome pricing is spreading through **services ownership, not software pricing pages**. The vendors that make it work either operate in a category with a native, cheap count (support) or become the services firm that owns the whole workflow (EvenUp's pre-litigation service, Crescendo's BPO model, RCM and accounting roll-ups). The Fin deal is the clearest market test so far. A strategic buyer that runs credits and seats paid about 9x for the category's outcome-priced leader, while venture investors paid about 100x for Sierra. That gap is the market's current estimate of how much the pricing model itself is worth. Two things to watch are whether Salesforce keeps $0.99 per outcome after closing, and whether any outcome-priced vendor discloses gross margins before inference costs per workflow rise as Gartner predicts.
